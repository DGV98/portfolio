# Resisting the Easy Button

My path into software engineering has not been linear. I graduated from college
as a Molecular Engineering major, a degree that the university was admittedly 
"testing out". I like to think of it as a glorified physics degree, as the bulk of 
my coursework was in the physics department. I enjoyed my time solving rigorous problems, 
as most of my problem sets involved thinking very hard for many hours to come up with a
solution. Trying things and failing, and trying all over again. I found that this was what I enjoyed
most about what I chose to study, but I quickly came to the realization that I would have to make
money in the outside world. I decided to pivot, somewhere in my third year. I started taking
CS classes, which allowed me to continue thinking and solving hard problems that 
scratched the itch in my brain, the part that wanted to *think*. 

## My First *Real* Job
I eventually found my first role in a consulting company as a software engineer and was able
to learn about cloud computing, albeit this role did not provide me with a lot of experience
(I did not work with any clients for the better part of 2 years). It was around this time that OpenAI came out
with GPT-4. I had not really reached for AI in hopes of trying to flesh out my knowledge.
I took [CS50](https://cs50.harvard.edu/x/), the introductory course Harvard offers, while expanding my skillset with their other classes.
{/*  David J. Malan did a fantastic job of distilling a lot of aspects 
of programming into these courses, making it easy for people to branch off into their interests.  */}
Because I had so much time to finish this course (my job *really* gave me nothing to do),
I decided to start experimenting with AI for my final project. I had used ChatGPT loosely to 
answer some random questions here and there, but I decided to start creating using their API. 
I made an app that allows you to create math practice problems for any category of math and filter it down
by difficulty. It was very simple and it allowed me to learn about web design. Throughout the process,
I used VSCode's copilot with GPT-4 to help me with designing and laying out the site. This made it really easy to 
code things without knowing the syntax or language itself. I was hooked.

## Today
Fast forward a couple of years, and it was hard to stop pushing the easy button. 
I did not stop pushing it in fact. I worked at several startups and organizations
where I simply became the [meat proxy](https://gruhn.me/blog/2026-08-03/) between the LLM and my boss. Even this 
portfolio site, I opened up my computer, type ```claude --dangerously-skip-permissions``` and let the LLM decide how 
to best represent my professional experience. 

*I stopped thinking.* 

Somewhere along the way, I stopped solving the hard problems. I stopped scratching 
that itch in my brain that wanted to think. Eventually I went back and tried doing some of the things
that I used to find easy, and I could not recall where to start. I tried solving math problems whose solutions
would come to me intuitively, and I could not. It was time for an intervention.
